Tabs

Each panel can hold several folders at once as tabs, so you can keep the places you use often just a click apart instead of navigating back and forth. Tabs belong to a single panel: the left and right panels each keep their own row of tabs, and switching or closing a tab only affects the active panel. A small tab bar appears above the file list, showing one chip per open tab plus a trailing "+" button.

Working with tabs

  1. Click a panel to make it active. New tabs always open in the active panel.
  2. Choose View > New Tab, or press Cmd+T, to open a new tab on the current folder. You can also click the "+" button at the end of the tab bar.
  3. Click any tab chip to switch to it, or press Cmd+} for the next tab and Cmd+{ for the previous one.
  4. To close the current tab, choose View > Close Tab or press Cmd+W. You can also middle-click a tab chip to close it. Choose View > Close All Tabs to clear all but the current one.

(Figure: The tab bar sits above the file list, with one chip per tab and a "+" button to open a new one.)

Open a folder in a new tab

  1. Move the cursor to a folder in the file list.
  2. Choose View > Open Folder in New Tab (Cmd+Shift+T) to open that folder in a new tab in the same panel.
  3. To open a tab without leaving the current one, choose View > New Tab in Background.

Lock a tab

Locking a tab keeps it pinned to its folder. Select the tab, then choose View > Lock Tab. A small lock icon appears on the tab chip. When a tab is locked, navigating into another folder opens that folder in a new tab instead of changing the locked one, so your pinned location stays put. Choose the menu item again to unlock it.

Shortcuts

Action Shortcut
New tab Cmd+T
New tab in background View > New Tab in Background
Open folder under cursor in a new tab Cmd+Shift+T
Close current tab Cmd+W
Next tab Cmd+}
Previous tab Cmd+{
Lock / unlock tab View > Lock Tab

Tips

  • Right-click a tab chip for quick actions such as duplicating or closing that tab.
  • Tabs are per panel. Use them to keep a working folder and a destination folder side by side, then switch between them without losing your place.
  • Locking is handy for a folder you return to often: you can browse away from it and still find it, unchanged, on its tab.
